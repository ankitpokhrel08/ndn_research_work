\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{NDN architecture and its security surface}
Named Data Networking replaces host-addressed delivery with retrieval by content name, and the NDN project overview \cite{zhang2010ndn} together with subsequent architectural surveys \cite{jaffri2013ndn} establishes the design that makes this possible: stateful per-Interest forwarding through the Pending Interest Table (PIT) and universal in-network caching through the Content Store (CS). Survey work on NDN caching \cite{caching_survey} documents how cache placement and replacement policies shape what is stored where, and how that same mechanism can be turned against the network by an adversary who controls what gets cached. Shah et al.\ survey the broader space of NDN security and integrity attacks, cataloguing threats from replay and Man-in-the-Middle attacks to content poisoning, and conclude that cache-based exploitation and stateful-forwarding exhaustion remain the two principal, structurally distinct attack surfaces unique to this architecture \cite{shah2023security}. Interest Flooding Attacks (IFA), which exhaust the PIT, and Cache Pollution (CP), which corrupts the CS, are consistently identified across this literature as the attacks with no direct counterpart in host-addressed networking, and they are the two attacks this paper studies.

\subsection{IFA, CP, and DDoS detection}
A separate line of work targets IFA and CP directly. Xu et al.\ propose a sketch-based mechanism for persistent DDoS detection in NDN, identifying malicious name prefixes with low computational and memory overhead \cite{xu_persistent_detection}. Liu et al.\ take a supervised approach, training an improved XGBoost classifier to detect hybrid attacks that combine Interest Flooding and Cache Pollution simultaneously \cite{liu2025xgboost}. Both lines of work illustrate a recurring pattern in this space. Sketch- and rule-based detectors rely on thresholds tuned to a fixed operating point, and an adversary who keeps each individual traffic feature within a plausible range, a low-rate or mimicry attack, can remain under that threshold indefinitely. Supervised detectors such as XGBoost avoid hand-tuned thresholds but substitute a different dependency: they require labelled attack traffic to train on, and their accuracy is not guaranteed to generalize to attack variants or rates absent from that training set. Just as importantly, both detectors, along with most of the literature they represent, are evaluated at a single attack rate chosen to be easily separable from background traffic. Neither the rule-based nor the supervised approach reports how detection degrades as the attacker throttles down, so neither establishes where detection actually fails.

\subsection{Isolation Forest and its application to NDN}
Isolation Forest, introduced by Liu et al., is an unsupervised anomaly detection algorithm that isolates anomalous points through random recursive partitioning; because anomalies are few and different, they require fewer splits to isolate than normal instances, giving the algorithm a natural anomaly score without any labelled training data \cite{liu2008isolation}. Its efficiency in high-dimensional feature spaces and its independence from labelled attack examples make it a natural fit for NDN, where attack traffic is scarce and diverse. Xing et al.\ apply exactly this idea in IFDM, an Isolation Forest-based defense against Interest Flooding Attacks, built on four features derived directly from PIT dynamics: Interest rate, Data rate, PIT entry count, and expired PIT entry count \cite{xing2021isolation}. IFDM reports improved detection accuracy and faster response than statistical threshold methods, and its results are the clearest prior evidence that unsupervised anomaly detection is viable in NDN. Its feature set, however, is built entirely around PIT exhaustion, the textbook signature of IFA, and its validation does not examine whether that signature persists under a real forwarding daemon's polling behaviour, nor whether the same feature set or the same detector transfers to Cache Pollution, an attack that does not touch the PIT at all.

\subsection{Simulation and emulation tooling}
The simulation platform used for the attacker-rate sweep in this paper, ndnSIM, is introduced by Mastorakis et al.\ as an ns-3-based simulator implementing the NDN forwarding pipeline in software \cite{mastorakis2015ndnsim}. It is among the most widely used tools for NDN experimentation in the literature reviewed above, and its role here is as a controlled, high-throughput complement to emulation on a real forwarding daemon.

\subsection{Gaps addressed by this paper}
Read together, this literature leaves four gaps unresolved. First, no prior work measures a detection floor: every evaluation surveyed above, sketch-based, supervised, or Isolation Forest-based, reports performance at a single attack rate rather than tracing the boundary between reliable detection and chance, so none establishes how quiet a successful attack can be while remaining stealthy. Second, the dominant unsupervised approach to IFA, IFDM, builds its feature set entirely from PIT-derived counters, encoding the textbook assumption that IFA manifests as PIT exhaustion without testing whether that signature survives contact with a real forwarding daemon's polling interval. Third, every detector reviewed here is validated on a single platform and, in most cases, a single topology, leaving open whether reported results reflect the attack itself or an artifact of one simulator's traffic model or one network's shape. Fourth, none of this work examines detection at the level of individual nodes, so none characterizes how an attack's effect propagates through a topology or which nodes carry the strongest detection signal. This paper closes all four gaps with a single unsupervised Isolation Forest detector, trained only on clean traffic, that measures the detection floor directly via an attacker-rate sweep, tests the PIT-exhaustion assumption against a real forwarding daemon, and is validated identically across two platforms and three topologies with per-node analysis throughout.
